# Travel Survival Kit Guide

## Overview

The Travel Survival Kit is a comprehensive pre-travel resource that ensures you have all essential information before arriving in any country. This guide covers 10 core travel components plus additional helpful resources for a seamless travel experience.

## Core Components

### 1. **SIM CARD SOLUTIONS**

**Purpose**: Obtain local mobile connectivity for calls, data, and internet access.

- **Budget-conscious?** → Compare per-GB cost across providers

## ADDITIONAL HELPFUL SECTIONS

### 11. **LOCAL CUSTOMS & ETIQUETTE**

A thorough etiquette section is non-negotiable in any Travel Survival Kit. Cultural missteps — even unintentional ones — can cause offence, deny entry to sacred sites, or make interactions unnecessarily difficult. Cover all subsections below for every destination.

#### 11a. Temple & Sacred Site Dress Code

Dress codes at religious and heritage sites are strictly enforced worldwide — research and communicate the exact rules for the destination.

**Standard requirements to verify and document:**

| **Rule**                | **What to Check**                            | **Enforcement Level**           |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **Shoulders covered**   | Sleeveless tops / tank tops prohibited       | Very strict at most temples     |
| **Knees covered**       | Shorts / mini-skirts prohibited              | Strictly enforced               |
| **Footwear**            | Shoes removed before entry                   | Mandatory at most Asian temples |
| **Head covering**       | Required at mosques, some temples            | Varies by faith/region          |
| **Colour restrictions** | White (mourning) or specific colours avoided | Culture-specific                |
| **Sarong/Wrap rental**  | Available at entrance for a small fee        | Common at major tourist temples |

> **Pro format tip:** Always state: _"T-shirts and lightweight long pants are the perfect temple outfit — simple, cool, and always accepted."_ Give travelers the positive solution, not just the list of prohibitions.

**Cambodia-specific rules:**

- Shoulders AND knees must be covered at all Angkor temples without exception. Guards at entry points actively check and will deny entry.
- Lightweight linen trousers and a cotton t-shirt are ideal in the heat. Avoid sleeveless shirts and short shorts entirely.
- Free sarong wraps are sometimes available to borrow but cannot be relied upon — dress correctly from the hotel.

#### 11b. The Head, Feet & Body — Cultural Hierarchy

Many Asian and South/Southeast Asian cultures observe a strict spiritual hierarchy of the human body. Get this wrong and the offence is serious.

**Universal rules to research per destination:**

- **The head is sacred**: Never touch another person on the head — not adults, not children. This applies in Thailand, Cambodia, Laos, Sri Lanka, and much of South and Southeast Asia.
- **The feet are considered the lowest and most impure part of the body**: Never point the soles of your feet at a person, a Buddha statue, a shrine, or a religious image. When sitting on the floor in a temple, tuck feet away from sacred objects and other people.
- **Passing objects**: Use both hands when giving or receiving items, especially in Japan and Korea. In many Buddhist countries, passing objects with the left hand alone is considered rude.

**Cambodia-specific:**

- Never touch a Cambodian person's head — even a child's. This is a significant cultural violation regardless of how affectionately it is intended.
- When seated near Buddha statues or monks, ensure the soles of your feet point away. Reclining with feet pointed toward the altar is deeply disrespectful.

#### 11c. Monk Interactions & Religious Etiquette

Monks are afforded the highest social respect in Buddhist countries. Specific rules apply that are not obvious to foreign visitors.

**Rules to document for Buddhist-majority destinations:**

| **Rule**                         | **Detail**                                                             | **Who It Applies To** |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| **Women cannot touch monks**     | No physical contact under any circumstances                            | All women             |
| **Women handing items to monks** | Must place item on a surface or table — never hand it directly         | All women             |
| **Men offering items**           | Can be handed directly but use both hands respectfully                 | All men               |
| **Photography of monks**         | Always ask permission first — many monks are comfortable, some are not | All visitors          |
| **Entering monastery grounds**   | Dress code applies — same as temple rules                              | All visitors          |
| **Morning alms-giving**          | Observe silently and respectfully — do not use flash photography       | All visitors          |

> **Cambodia-specific:** Women must never touch a monk or hand anything directly to one. If a woman needs to give something to a monk, she should place it on a nearby table or surface and allow the monk to pick it up.

#### 11d. Saving Face & Conflict Resolution

"Saving face" is a central social concept across most of East, Southeast, and South Asia. Public embarrassment — for either party — is to be avoided at all costs.

**Key rules:**

- **Never raise your voice, shout, or show visible anger in public** — regardless of how frustrated you are. A calm demeanour is always more effective and earns respect.
- **Smile through difficult negotiations** — in Cambodia, Thailand, and much of the region, a genuine smile is the most powerful negotiating tool a visitor has.
- **Public confrontations always backfire** — if a vendor, driver, or official makes an error, address it quietly and privately. A public accusation humiliates the other person and ends any chance of resolution.
- **"Yes" does not always mean yes** — in many Asian cultures, saying "no" directly is considered impolite. A vague answer, a smile, or a change of subject may mean "no." Read context carefully.
- **Patience is a virtue with practical value** — losing patience visibly signals weakness and often results in worse outcomes. Those who remain calm, polite, and persistent are far more successful.

> **Cambodia-specific:** Showing anger or creating a public scene is deeply embarrassing and will not get you what you want. Always maintain a calm, smiling demeanor when negotiating or resolving issues — in markets, with drivers, and especially with any officials.

#### 11e. Responsible Tourism & Child Welfare

This section is especially important in developing-country destinations and must be included prominently.

**Core principles:**

- **Do not give money, candy, or gifts to begging children at temples or tourist sites.** This directly incentivises parents and local networks to keep children out of school and working the tourist circuit instead. The kindness feels real but causes lasting harm.
- **Support legitimate alternatives instead:**
  - Eat at NGO-run training restaurants (in Cambodia: Romdeng, Friends/Mith Samlanh, Marum)
  - Buy crafts from certified fair-trade cooperatives
  - Donate to verified local organisations rather than individuals on the street
- **Orphanage tourism is harmful:** Many "orphanages" in developing countries are not genuine — children are recruited from living families to attract tourist donations. Do not visit or donate to orphanages.
- **Animal welfare:** Do not ride elephants (harmful), visit venues with performing wildlife, or pay for photos with captive animals (tiger temples, chained monkeys, etc.).
- **Ask before photographing people** — especially children, monks, and market vendors. A smile and a gesture asking permission takes two seconds and is always the right choice.

**Where to direct travelers for legitimate giving:**

- NGO training restaurants (research per country)
- Certified fair-trade craft cooperatives
- Verified organisations with transparent financials (Charity Navigator, GiveWell listings)

#### 11f. General Social Customs & Greetings

Cover the standard social customs relevant to the destination:

- **Greeting style**: Wai (Thailand/Cambodia), Namaste (India/Nepal), bow (Japan/Korea), handshake (Western contexts within the destination), etc.
- **Tipping culture**: Is tipping expected, optional, or offensive? At what rate? In which settings?
- **Dress outside temples**: Is modesty expected generally in the culture, or only at religious sites?
- **Photography restrictions**: Any government buildings, military sites, or individuals where photography is prohibited?
- **Public displays of affection**: What is socially acceptable in public spaces?
- **Bargaining norms**: Where is bargaining expected and where is it rude?
- **Dining etiquette**: Chopsticks, spoon + fork, hands — what is correct? Sharing dishes, ordering customs, bill-splitting norms.

### 12. **SAFETY & PRACTICAL INFORMATION**

Safety and health information must be specific, actionable, and honest. Vague reassurances ("generally safe") are not useful. Give travelers real data and real guidance.

#### 12a. Vaccinations & Preventative Health

Always verify current WHO and destination-country health recommendations. The following framework applies to most tropical and developing-country destinations.

**Vaccination research checklist:**

| **Vaccine / Medication**           | **Priority Level**   | **Notes**                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Hepatitis A**                    | Strongly recommended | Transmitted through contaminated food/water — essential for any developing country                  |
| **Hepatitis B**                    | Recommended          | Especially for longer stays or those in medical/healthcare settings                                 |
| **Typhoid**                        | Recommended          | High risk in areas with poor sanitation and street food consumption                                 |
| **Tetanus / Diphtheria (Td/Tdap)** | Recommended          | Ensure booster is current (within 10 years)                                                         |
| **Japanese Encephalitis**          | Destination-specific | Required for rural/agricultural areas and longer stays in Southeast Asia                            |
| **Rabies pre-exposure**            | Destination-specific | Recommended for adventurous travel, wildlife contact, or areas with limited post-exposure treatment |
| **Malaria prophylaxis**            | Destination-specific | Required for some rural zones; generally NOT needed for major tourist hubs and cities               |
| **Cholera**                        | Destination-specific | High-risk flood/disaster zones only                                                                 |

> **Cambodia-specific:** Hepatitis A, Typhoid, and Tetanus are recommended for all visitors. Malaria prophylaxis is generally NOT required for Phnom Penh, Siem Reap, and main tourist corridors — consult a travel doctor for rural/border regions. Always bring DEET-based insect repellent regardless of malaria risk.

**Always advise travelers to:**

- Consult a travel medicine clinic or GP 6-8 weeks before departure
- Carry a small first-aid kit (antihistamine, rehydration sachets, antiseptic, blister plasters, diarrhoea medication)
- Know their travel insurance emergency evacuation coverage

#### 12b. Mosquito-Borne Illness & Insect Protection

Mosquito-borne diseases are among the most significant health risks for travelers to tropical destinations. Cover this section clearly.

**Key diseases to flag per destination:**

| **Disease**      | **Mosquito Type**        | **Peak Risk**               | **Prevention**                                            |
| ---------------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Dengue Fever** | Aedes (daytime biting)   | Rainy season                | DEET repellent, covered clothing, mosquito nets           |
| **Malaria**      | Anopheles (night biting) | Year-round in endemic zones | Anti-malaria medication, DEET, nets                       |
| **Zika Virus**   | Aedes                    | Rainy season                | DEET, covered clothing (important for pregnant travelers) |
| **Chikungunya**  | Aedes                    | Rainy season                | DEET, covered clothing                                    |

> **Cambodia-specific:** DEET-based mosquito repellent is mandatory — not optional. Dengue Fever is a real and present risk year-round in Cambodia, including in cities. Apply repellent morning and evening without exception.

#### 12c. Water & Food Safety

Water and food safety rules vary dramatically by destination and must be stated plainly.

**Water safety framework:**

| **Source**                  | **Safety**               | **Guidance**                                                                                      |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tap water**               | Research per destination | In most of Southeast Asia, South Asia, Africa, Latin America: unsafe. Do not drink.               |
| **Sealed bottled water**    | Safe                     | Standard recommendation for unsafe-water destinations                                             |
| **Ice in restaurants**      | Varies                   | In tourist restaurants: usually made from filtered water. In local markets: risk of contamination |
| **Filtered water stations** | Safe                     | High-capacity filtered dispensers at hotels/hostels — refill personal bottles                     |
| **Boiled water**            | Safe                     | Reliable if fully boiled                                                                          |

> **Cambodia-specific:** Tap water is completely unsafe throughout Cambodia. Do not drink it, do not use it to brush teeth. Rely exclusively on sealed bottled water (widely available, ₹500–2,000 KHR per litre) or use hotel-provided filtered water stations. In restaurants, confirm ice is from filtered sources if sensitive.

**Food safety guidance (research per destination):**

- Street food from busy, high-turnover stalls is generally safer than quiet tourist restaurants (freshness is the key variable)
- Avoid raw salads, unpeeled raw fruit, and raw shellfish in high-risk destinations
- Eat food that is freshly cooked and served hot
- Carry oral rehydration sachets — traveller's diarrhoea is common and dehydration is the main risk

#### 12d. Personal Safety & Crime Awareness

Honest, specific safety information — neither alarmist nor dismissive. Give travelers the real picture.

**Framework for each destination:**

- **Overall safety rating**: Low / Moderate / High risk for tourists
- **Most common issues**: What actually happens to tourists (petty theft, scams, bag snatching, drink spiking, etc.)
- **High-risk areas**: Specific neighbourhoods or situations to avoid
- **Safe areas**: Where tourists can move freely
- **Transport safety**: Which transport types are safe vs. risky (unlicensed taxis, motorbike taxis, etc.)
- **Night safety**: Specific guidance for moving around after dark
- **Scam awareness**: The most common scams at this destination and how to avoid them

**How to document each safety issue:**

```
ISSUE: [Name of risk — e.g. "Bag-snatching from motorbikes"]
WHERE IT HAPPENS: [Specific areas or situations]
HOW IT HAPPENS: [Brief description]
HOW TO AVOID IT: [Specific preventive action]
WHAT TO DO IF IT HAPPENS: [Report to tourist police / nearest embassy / insurance company]
```

> **Cambodia-specific:**
>
> - Violent crime against tourists is extremely rare.
> - **Most common issue:** Bag-snatching by passing motorbikes, primarily in Phnom Penh. Keep bags strapped across the body and worn on the side away from the road. Do not hold phones or cameras loosely on busy streets.
> - Tuk-tuk and motorbike taxi scams (overcharging, incorrect destinations) are common — agree price before boarding or use a ride-hailing app.
> - Gem scams ("lucky winner" schemes) target tourists in major cities — ignore all unsolicited offers.

## HOW TO USE THIS SKILL

### For Creating Country Guides

1. **Research the 10 core sections** first
2. **Gather 3-4 reliable sources** for each section
3. **Verify current pricing and hours** (things change seasonally)
4. **Include both budget and luxury options** in each category
5. **Add personal touches** (pro tips, unusual recommendations)
6. **Organize by traveler type** where applicable (backpacker, luxury, family, solo)

### Best Practices

- **Always include WHERE and HOW**: Don't just name a place; add address, directions, access info
- **Price transparency**: Show costs in local currency AND estimated USD/INR for international travelers
- **Real opening hours**: Confirm hours, as many places have seasonal variations
- **Practical tips**: What locals know that tourists don't
- **Avoid tourist traps**: Warn about overpriced/inauthentic options
- **Contact information**: Phone numbers, websites where applicable
- **Multiple options**: Provide budget, mid-range, and premium choices in each category

## IMPORTANT REMINDERS

⚠️ **Always Verify**: Prices, hours, and availability change seasonally
⚠️ **Use Local Currency**: Mention conversion rates but always in original currency
⚠️ **Include Alternatives**: Not all travelers want the same experience
⚠️ **Pro Tips Are Gold**: Personal recommendations beat generic info
⚠️ **Safety First**: Mention any travel warnings or unsafe areas
⚠️ **Authenticity Matters**: Distinguish tourist spots from local favorites
⚠️ **Transportation Context**: Always explain HOW to get somewhere, not just WHERE
⚠️ **Etiquette Is Essential**: Dress codes, head/feet rules, monk interactions, and saving face norms must be covered for every destination — not treated as optional
⚠️ **Health & Safety Must Be Specific**: "Generally safe" is not useful. Name the actual risks (Dengue, bag-snatching, unsafe tap water), give exact prevention steps (DEET 30%+, sealed bottled water, body-side bag straps), and state what to do if something goes wrong
